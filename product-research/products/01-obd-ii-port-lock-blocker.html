1 · Product overview

Problem solved
Thieves program new keys via OBD port; blocker physically prevents access.
Who experiences it / frequency
Owners of keyless-entry cars, esp. Toyota/Mazda/Hyundai/Kia in Israel. — One-time install
Current solutions & gaps
Immobilizers, relay-attack pouches; OBD locks are niche and brand-specific; weak awareness.
Target audience
Security-conscious car owners, garages/installers
Why now
Israeli police data (Lahav 433) and Pointer's 2023 analysis both name key-cloning through the OBD socket as the leading theft method, often with a cellular jammer; after two years of falling thefts, attempts spiked about 25% in early 2026 (bhol, Feb 2026). No Israeli retailer sells a bolt-on port lock: Zap, ACE and KSP return only steering-wheel locks at ₪116–517 and a locksmith-installed OBD 'safe' at ₪599.

Skeptical review (Agent 7)
Demand evidence is stronger than the file suggests for Israel specifically: Pointer (2023) and a 2026 bhol report say the leading Israeli theft method is key-cloning through the OBD socket, often with a cellular jammer, and Lahav 433 reported a ~25% spike in attempts in early 2026 after two years of decline. But forum users (4Runner, Lexus UK) argue a port lock does not stop CAN-injection and can be bypassed by probing the CAN wires behind the lock, and it blocks garage diagnostics; fit is vehicle-specific (port position/orientation differs by model). Supplier price is plausible: Alibaba shows an aluminium OBD port lock at $6.25-7 (MOQ 1) and cap-style at $11.50-14.90 (MOQ 10). No Israeli retail comp exists at all: Zap/ACE/KSP return only steering-wheel locks (116-517 ILS); the 599 ILS comp is a locksmith-installed OBD 'safe' (Akeyless-type), not a bolt-on cap, so 199 ILS is untested. Patents exist (US app 20240413563 'OBD II Port Lock', US 11999230/12600320 adapter-with-notification, Zammitek 'patented' steel cover), so the IP level should be medium, not low; a generic screw-cap design is probably safe but must not copy a specific patented mechanism.
Risk flags: no Israeli retail comparable found; vehicle-specific fit -> return risk; effectiveness disputed (CAN injection bypass); design/utility patents exist in US/EU; blocks workshop diagnostics - needs clear labelling
Model overrides applied
Real Israeli theft-vector demand but zero retail price anchor, vehicle-fit returns risk and patent density; order samples for the 5 best-selling Israeli models (Toyota/Hyundai/Kia/Mazda/Skoda) before committing.
Import & regulatory risk summary
Low regulatory burden (passive metal part, no official standard expected; Hebrew labelling + importer details). Duty line 8301 (locks) to confirm, modelled 7%. IP: medium — generic design only.

Primary customer profile
Owners of the most-stolen models in Israel (Toyota, Mazda, Hyundai/Kia, Honda), fleet/lease drivers, parents buying for adult children; 30–55, insurance-aware, already own a steering lock.
Marketing angles
  1. 'The thief plugs in, not breaks in': 30-second explainer of OBD key cloning with the lock stopping it (fear + simple fix).
  2. 'Costs less than one month of the insurance excess': price anchored against ₪1,500–3,000 deductibles and the ₪599 installed alternative.
  3. Anti-theft kit: OBD lock + steering lock bundle for the top-10 stolen models, with a Hebrew fit guide per model.
